Get set up

Two new tools today

slice_sample()
Picks rows at random. slice_sample(gss, n = 50) gives a random sample of 50 people. Each time you run it, you get a different 50.
replicate()
Repeats a command many times and keeps every result. replicate(500, ...) draws 500 samples in a few seconds.

One sample at a time: Steps 2 to 4

Step 2: the answer in the full data

Run mean(gss$relig == "None"). The $ sign picks one column out of the data, so gss$relig is the religion column. As in Lab 1, the mean of TRUE and FALSE answers is the share for whom it is TRUE.

Checkpoint. The Console shows [1] 0.1639901: 16.4 percent of the 21,483 people report no religion. Write it down. Today it is the true value you compare every sample to.

Step 3: one random sample of 50

  1. Put 50 in the first blank and run the line. Nothing prints. R stored 50 randomly chosen people as my_sample.
  2. Put my_sample in the next blank and run it. Write down the share.

Step 4: two more samples

Run the two Step 3 lines again, twice. Each time R draws a new random sample of 50 and gives a new share. Write down all three. Compare them with the person next to you. Your numbers will not match, and neither of you made an error.

500 samples: Steps 5 and 6

Step 5: 500 samples of 50

Step 5 has no blanks. Select every line from shares_50 <- down to print(chart_50) and run them together. Each bar shows how many of the 500 samples gave a share in that range. The dashed line is the true value, 16.4 percent.

Your chart will look a little different from this one, because your 500 samples are different. The shape will be about the same: centered near the dashed line, with a wide spread.

Step 6: 500 samples of 1,000

  1. Put 1000 in the blank (no comma), then select every line from shares_1000 <- down to print(chart_1000) and run them.
  2. Compare the two charts. Both use the same horizontal axis, so you can see the difference directly.
The samples of 1,000 still differ from each other, but far less. This spread from sample to sample is called sampling variation. It shrinks as samples get bigger, and it is the reason a poll reports a margin of error.

A list that leaves people out: Step 7

Suppose the only list you could sample from included people aged 40 and older, the way the Literary Digest's lists in 1936 left out many people. You draw 500 samples of 2,000 people from that list, twice the size of Step 6.

Before you run Step 7: where will these 500 samples of 2,000 center? The full-data share is 16.4 percent.
16.5%
  1. Put 40 in the blank and run the frame_40 line. The >= sign means "greater than or equal to."
  2. Select every line from shares_frame <- down to print(chart_frame) and run them.
The samples are large and agree closely with each other, and every one of them is wrong in the same direction. Younger adults are more likely to report no religion, and the list left them out. A bigger sample shrinks sampling variation. It cannot bring back people the list never included.
