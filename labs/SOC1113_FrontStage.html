Goffman said social life is theater: you stage a performance of yourself for every audience you face. He wrote that in 1959, about dinner parties and shop counters. This page tests the theory against the platform where your generation performs most, using published survey data on what American teens post, what they refuse to post, and what the performance costs. Then a simulator puts you in the manager's chair and takes your separate audiences away. Guess before you reveal. The gaps between your guesses and the data are part of the lesson.

A 34-point gap, and Goffman predicts it. You see everyone else's front stage and compare it with your own back stage, the one place the staging is visible to you. The simulator: your audiences, merged

Offline, your audiences arrive one at a time, and you stage a different performance for each. Goffman called that audience segregation. Platforms remove it: one post, every audience at once. Researchers call the result context collapse. Run it on yourself. For each post below, check the audiences you would let see it. Then merge them.

The postClose friendsFamilyA boss or coachEveryone at school

This is a mechanism demonstration, not survey data. The only numbers in it are your own checkboxes.

What people actually do about it: run a second account and rebuild the segregation by hand, post things so vague no audience can object, or go quiet and lurk. Each is a rational stage-management response, and each has a cost. The survey tabs on this page are what those costs look like measured at national scale.

The term context collapse comes from research by Alice Marwick and danah boyd (2011) on how social platforms flatten multiple audiences into one, building on Goffman's audience segregation. The simulator demonstrates the mechanism only. It records nothing.

Questions to take with you

  1. In the simulator, which post lost the most audiences when the merge came? What does that post have in common with the topics teens keep off the feed on the first tab?
  2. The edit tab shows 27% of teens performing for schools and employers who are not watching yet. Is a performance for a future audience still impression management, or something new that Goffman did not describe? Defend your answer.
  3. Girls 15 to 17 do the most editing and report the most pressure and drama. Using this chapter and the socialization chapter, explain the gap sociologically rather than psychologically.
  4. The 34-point gap on the everyone-else tab: explain it in Goffman's terms in two sentences, as if to a friend who thinks everyone else really is doing better.
  5. Where is your back stage, the place you drop the act? Now the hard question: name one way even that space has a script and an audience.
  6. If the self is a set of performances, is there anything underneath them? Goffman's answer was unsettling. Give yours, in three sentences.
