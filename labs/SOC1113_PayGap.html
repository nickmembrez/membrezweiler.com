Everyone has heard that women earn less than men. Almost everyone is wrong about why. This page holds the published numbers: the gap itself, what survives when you compare identical jobs, who works where, and how far women rise. Every figure is hidden until you commit a guess, and the tab most worth arguing with is the second one, where the gap comes apart into pieces that point at the structure of work itself. That is the finding that won Claudia Goldin the Nobel Prize, and by the end of the page you will know what she meant.

1The gap mostly is not same-job, same-pay

Payscale's 2025 analysis: controlling for job, qualifications, and experience, women earn 99 cents on the male dollar. That last cent is real and it is illegal, but it cannot explain a 17-cent gap. So the interesting question moves: if it is not mostly unequal pay for equal work, where do the other 16 cents live?

2They live in which jobs, and which hours

Men and women hold different jobs (next tab measures how different), and the female-skewed ones pay less. On top of the sorting sits the hours structure: the highest-paying roles pay a premium for long, inflexible, always-available time. Goldin calls them greedy jobs. Anyone who cannot promise the always turns down the premium, whatever their talent.

3The timing gives it away

Remember the age rows on the first tab: near parity in the 20s, then the slide. The gap opens exactly when caregiving arrives, and it opens asymmetrically. Payscale 2025: mothers earn 75 cents per fathers' dollar, while fathers out-earn even men without children at home, the fatherhood premium Pew documents. The same event moves the two sexes' earnings in opposite directions. That is a structure operating, not a talent difference appearing at age 30 on schedule.

4The verdict

The pay gap is mostly not a story about biased managers writing unequal paychecks for identical work. It is a story about which occupations sort whom, how the biggest rewards attach to inflexible hours, and who absorbs caregiving when it collides with those hours. That is why Goldin's Nobel-winning conclusion is structural: redesign how work rewards time, and the gap moves. Wait for attitudes to improve, and it stalls, which is exactly what the last twenty years look like.

Both mistakes are wrong. The gap is not a myth: 83 cents compounds across a career into hundreds of thousands of dollars, and the 1-cent controlled gap is still measurable discrimination. And the gap is not proof that every employer pays women less for the same work: the data say most of it is built earlier, in the sorting and the hours. Keeping both halves of that sentence is the exam skill.

Two findings hide in these bars. First, the sorting is enormous, and Chapter 5 explained how it starts: toys, praise, channeled interests, agents of socialization steering the doors people walk through long before any hiring manager appears. Structure shapes the choices, so choice and structure are not opposites. Second, the female-skewed jobs pay less at similar skill levels, and the sharpest evidence is longitudinal: sociologists tracking occupations across decades (Levanon, England, and Allison, using census data from 1950 to 2000) found that when a field's gender mix shifts toward women, its relative pay tends to fall. The wage follows who is doing the work, not just the work.

Questions to take with you

  1. Which guess missed by the most, and in which direction? The controlled-gap guess is the revealing one: what story had you been carrying about where the gap lives?
  2. Explain to a skeptical friend why "99 cents in the same job" and "the pay gap is real and large" are both true in one paragraph, without contradicting yourself.
  3. Run the occupational sorting through Chapter 5: name the agents of socialization that steered you toward or away from a career door, and one moment you can actually remember it happening.
  4. The devaluation finding says pay follows who does the work. What does that predict for nursing and teaching as more men enter them, and for software as more women do? How would you test it?
  5. Goldin says redesigning how work rewards time would shrink the gap. Name one concrete redesign in your intended field, and who would resist it.
  6. Intersectionality check: this page mostly compares all women with all men. Which numbers would you want broken out by race to see whose gap is widest, and what do you predict?
