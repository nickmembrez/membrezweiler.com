Robert Merton published a nine-page argument in 1938 that still organizes this part of criminology. American culture, he said, prescribes the same goal to everybody, and American social structure hands out the legitimate means of reaching it very unevenly. That mismatch is the strain, and it does not require anyone to be poorly socialized, badly parented, or short on self-control. It only requires a society that tells everyone to win and then rations who is allowed to. This page treats that as a claim you can check rather than a slogan, because unusually good data exists on exactly the thing Merton was describing.

The promise, and whether it was kept

The specific promise inside the American Dream is that you will do better than your parents did. That is a testable claim, and Raj Chetty and colleagues tested it by linking tax records to census data and asking, for each birth cohort, what share of children were earning more at age 30 than their parents had earned at the same age.

Start with the generation that came of age after the Second World War.

Roughly a coin flip. For the cohort born in 1940, doing better than your parents was close to a guarantee. For children born in the 1980s it became about as likely as not. Notice what this does to Merton's argument. He assumed a culture that promises success to everyone. He did not need to assume that the promise had gotten harder to keep, and it has.

Before you conclude the economy just stopped growing

The obvious explanation is slower growth. The authors tested it directly by running two counterfactuals: give the 1980s cohort the GDP growth rates of the 1940s cohort, or give them the way that growth was distributed in the 1940s. Only one of those fixes it.

The pie kept growing. The slices changed. This is as close as social science gets to a direct measurement of Merton's structural claim. The cultural goal did not move. What moved was the distribution of the legitimate means of reaching it, which is precisely the mismatch he named in 1938 and precisely the thing he said would generate pressure toward what he called innovation.

One honesty note before you use this in an argument. Everything on this page is about income mobility, not about crime. Chetty and colleagues were not testing strain theory and did not claim to. What this data establishes is that the PREMISE of Merton's theory describes the United States accurately. Whether the mismatch actually produces crime is a separate question, and it is the question the rest of the chapter is about. Treating a well-measured premise as if it were a demonstrated cause is exactly the mistake this course keeps asking you not to make.

The ladder is not the same height everywhere

Merton's claim is about structure, which means it should show up as variation across places rather than as a fact about individuals. So take the sharpest version of the question. If a child grows up in a family in the bottom fifth of the income distribution, what are the odds that child ends up in the top fifth as an adult?

If opportunity were perfectly equal, the answer would be 20 percent everywhere, because a fifth of adults are in the top fifth by definition. Guess the national figure.

A child in one American metro is roughly three times as likely to make that climb as a child in another. Same country, same culture, same advertising, same message about what a successful life looks like. Different rungs. If you wanted to draw a picture of what Merton meant by universal goals and rationed means, this is close to it.

What the geography does and does not tell you. These are the odds for children raised in a place, not a property of the place itself, and the causes are genuinely contested. Later work by the same team found that moving younger children to higher-mobility areas improved their adult outcomes, which is evidence that place is doing some causal work rather than simply sorting families. It is still not a finding about crime, and a low-mobility metro is not thereby a high-crime metro. Hold the two separately.

Five ways to live with the mismatch

Merton's most cited contribution is a table. Given a cultural goal and a set of approved means, a person can accept or reject each one, and the combinations produce five ways of adapting. Only one of them is what we usually call conformity, and only one of them is where most property crime lives.

Conformity
Accepts the goal, accepts the means. Keep striving the approved way. Most people, most of the time.
Innovation
Accepts the goal, rejects the means. Keep the destination, improvise the route. This is the criminological star.
Ritualism
Rejects the goal, keeps the means. Stops believing in the climb and follows the procedure anyway.
Retreatism
Rejects both. Withdrawal from the whole arrangement.
Rebellion
Replaces both. Not opting out, but substituting a different goal and a different set of means.

Agnew: strain is bigger than money

Merton's strain is one specific thing: you want the thing everyone wants and cannot get it legitimately. Robert Agnew argued in 1992 that this is far too narrow, and that strain should cover the whole range of negative relationships people find themselves in. He proposed three types, and the mechanism running through all of them is emotional rather than economic. Strain produces negative emotion, anger above all, and crime is one way of coping with that emotion. Most people cope some other way.

Failing to reach a goal
Money, status, respect, autonomy, fairness. Merton's strain is one member of this category rather than the whole story.
Losing something valued
A breakup, a death, an eviction, a job, a friendship. Removal of positively valued stimuli.
Being subjected to something bad
Abuse, bullying, harassment, discrimination, chronic conflict. Presentation of negative stimuli.

The critique, and Agnew's answer to it

Broadening a theory has a cost. If almost any bad experience counts as strain, and most strained people do not offend, then what exactly would refute the theory? That is a real falsifiability problem and Chapter 1 taught you to be suspicious of it.

Agnew answered it directly in 2001 by specifying which strains should matter. He argued that strains lead to crime when they are seen as unjust, high in magnitude, associated with low social control, and when they create some pressure or incentive to engage in criminal coping. That is a testable narrowing, and it produced a list. Before you look at it, predict one thing.

Two strains with numbers attached

Strain theory is easy to state and hard to measure, because most strains live inside people's lives rather than in administrative records. Here are two that leave a paper trail.

Legal financial obligations

A criminal case in the United States generates fines, court costs, supervision fees, and surcharges that follow a person for years after the sentence itself is finished. In Agnew's terms this stacks all three strain types at once: it removes resources, it presents a continuing negative condition, and it blocks goals that require a clean record or a driver's license. It is also, unusually, a strain the state creates on purpose.

Menendez, M., Eisen, L., Atchison, N., and Crowley, M. (2019). The Steep Costs of Criminal Justice Fees and Fines. Brennan Center for Justice. The report also found that roughly half of families with a convicted member cannot afford to pay the fees and fines imposed. Retrieved August 2026.

Read those first two numbers together. A system that spends 41 cents to collect a dollar, and fails to collect nearly half of what it imposes, is not functioning as a revenue system. What it reliably produces instead is durable debt attached to people who already have the least capacity to absorb it. Whatever else that is, it is a machine for manufacturing exactly the conditions general strain theory says lead to crime, which is an awkward thing for a criminal justice system to be doing.

Keep the inference honest. These figures document the scale and inefficiency of the debt. They do not by themselves show that the debt causes offending. The strain argument is a prediction about what such conditions should produce, and it is a plausible one, but the numbers on this page are about the strain and not about the crime.

Discrimination as strain

Agnew's 2001 specification named experiences with prejudice and discrimination as a strain likely to lead to crime, on the reasoning that it scores high on every one of his four characteristics: it is unjust by definition, often severe, frequently outside the reach of any authority that would intervene, and it generates anger with no legitimate target. That is a prediction, and it has been tested.
