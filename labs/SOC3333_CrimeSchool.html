Last chapter argued that crime needs no teaching, only a failure of restraint. This chapter argues the opposite: that crime is learned the way everything else is learned, from the people closest to you, and that the pushing is the whole story. Both camps agree on one stubborn fact. People who offend have friends who offend, and that association is one of the strongest correlates in the entire field. They disagree completely about what it means. This page works through that disagreement, then shows you what happened when someone took learning theory seriously enough to build programs on it. The last two tabs are a review aid for the exam.

One fact, two theories, no agreement

Take an ordinary finding: adolescents who report delinquent friends report more delinquency themselves. Nobody disputes it. Here is what the two camps say it means.

Control theory says
The bond broke first. Weak attachment to parents and school left these kids free to drift toward each other. The friendship is a symptom, not a cause, and the causal work was already done before they met.
Learning theory says
The friendship is the cause. Techniques, opportunities, and above all definitions favorable to law-breaking are transmitted in intimate groups. The friends taught them, exactly as friends teach everything else.

Criminologists call this the selection versus socialization problem, and it is the single most important methodological argument in this chapter. Before we get to the answer, commit to a number.

Read this the way you read the Chapter 8 scorecard. The two components Sutherland gave the theory in 1947, association and definitions, are the ones carrying it. The two components Akers added in the 1960s, reinforcement and imitation, are less than half as strong. The authors said outright that the imitation portion may not be well supported by the existing literature. So the theory works, and it works mostly because of its oldest parts.

How this compares. Chapter 8 gave you self-control at mean correlations of about .26 attitudinal and .28 behavioral. Differential association sits at .225. A 2019 review of the peer literature put it this way: the effects of delinquent peer associations are at least comparable to, if not larger than, the effects of other well-known correlates of crime such as self-control. Two theories that call each other wrong have produced predictors of roughly the same size, measured by different people over fifty years. That is worth sitting with.

What happens when you take learning theory seriously

Here is a genuine test. If crime is learned in intimate groups through definitions favorable to law-breaking, then deliberately gathering delinquent young people into a group should teach them more crime, not less. That is an uncomfortable prediction, it is unambiguous, and it has been tested many times, because a great deal of juvenile programming does exactly that.

Researchers named the mechanism deviancy training: within a group of high-risk peers, talk about rule-breaking gets laughter, attention, and status, which is textbook differential reinforcement. Take the most famous example, the programs in which young people are brought into a prison to be confronted by incarcerated adults. Guess what the randomized trials found before you look.

Retrieved August 2026.

An odds ratio above 1 means the program group did worse. Every analysis the reviewers ran pointed the same direction. Their conclusion was that jurisdictions already running these programs should reevaluate them, because they are at risk of causing harm to the very citizens they aim to help. This is one of the clearest cases in all of criminal justice of an intervention that is popular, intuitive, cheap, and actively counterproductive.

Be careful about what this does and does not prove. These trials show that the programs made things worse. They do not by themselves prove that deviancy training was the mechanism, because a confrontational prison visit could backfire for several reasons at once. What makes the learning explanation credible is that the same harmful pattern shows up across a wider family of interventions that share one feature: they put high-risk young people in a room together. That is the pattern a 1999 review in American Psychologist called attention to under the heading of when interventions harm.

Now put this beside last week. Chapter 8 showed you a theory whose weakest strand, involvement, predicted correctly that keeping young people busy would not reduce crime. Chapter 9 shows you a theory that predicted correctly that grouping them would increase it. Two rival theories, two successful predictions, both about programs that sound obviously good. Theory is not decoration. It is the thing that would have told you, in advance and for free, that these were bad bets.

The five techniques of neutralization

Gresham Sykes and David Matza argued in 1957 that most people who break rules do not reject the rules. They excuse themselves from them, in advance, using a small and surprisingly standard set of moves. Matza called the resulting state drift: not committed to crime, not committed to conformity, floating between them and released episodically by whichever excuse is available.

This is also the exam-relevant bridge back to Sutherland, because these five are what definitions favorable to law-breaking look like when you catch them in the act. Sort each statement into the technique it uses.

Second, notice how ordinary all five are. You have used every one of them this month about something, which is exactly the point Matza was making. Drift is the normal condition, not a deviant one.
