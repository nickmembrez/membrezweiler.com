This page is about the distance between what Americans believe about crime and what the data show. Chapter 1 says criminology is the scientific study of crime, which only matters if science and common sense disagree. They do, and you are about to measure the disagreement on yourself. First, a fact. Then, your guess.

Here is the fact: in 2025 the United States recorded what the FBI called the largest one-year drop in violent crime and murder since it began keeping national records in 1937. Murder fell an estimated 18.1 percent in a single year. That October, while the drop was underway, Gallup asked Americans the question it has asked since 1989: is there more crime in the U.S. than there was a year ago, or less?

Crime, deviance, both, or neither?

Chapter 1 draws a line between crime (an act that violates criminal law) and deviance (an act that violates social norms). The two overlap, but neither contains the other. Sort each act below. Some have a clean answer and some are genuinely contested, and the contested ones teach the most.

What the sort shows. The legalistic definition gives you a crisp rule, an act is a crime when a criminal statute forbids it, but it stays silent about which acts get statutes and which statutes get enforced. The interactionist definition says crime is whatever a society successfully labels as crime, which explains why your answers changed with jurisdiction, era, and enforcement. Sutherland's three domains are all here: the making of laws (who wrote the wage theft rules?), the breaking of laws (everyone speeds), and reactions to law-breaking (who actually gets arrested?).

The same act, wandering across the line

In 1969, Gallup asked Americans for the first time whether marijuana use should be legal. Twelve percent said yes. The act has not changed since 1969. The chemistry has not changed. What changed is the label, which is the interactionist point in one drug.

Support was 12 percent in 1969. What percent of Americans said marijuana should be legal in Gallup's 2025 survey?

64 percent, and the law followed the norm. Majority support arrived in 2013, right after Colorado and Washington voted to legalize. Today marijuana is legal for adults in roughly half the states and still a crime in Texas. Notice the 2025 number also dipped from the 2023 record, a reminder that moral boundaries move in both directions. Mala prohibita acts, wrong because prohibited, are exactly the acts where you should expect the line to wander. Nobody expects a Gallup rebound for robbery.

Sources: Gallup, "Grassroots Support for Legalizing Marijuana Hits Record 70%" (1969: 12%, 2013: 58%, 2023: 70%) and Gallup 2025 survey as reported by NORML, Nov 2025 (64%, down from 68% in 2024).
