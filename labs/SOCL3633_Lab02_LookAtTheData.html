Fill in and run: Steps 2 to 5

These steps answer three questions about the data: how many people are in it, what was recorded about each person, and how many people were surveyed in each year.

Step 2: how big is the data?

  1. Fill in both blanks with gss:
    nrow(gss)
ncol(gss)
  2. Run each line with Ctrl+Enter.
Checkpoint. The Console shows [1] 21483 and then [1] 9. The data has 21,483 rows and 9 columns. Each row stands for one person who answered the survey. Each column is one variable: one survey question, or one fact recorded about the person.

Step 3: what is in each column?

Run glimpse(gss). Each line of the output is one variable, with its type and its first few values. In the tvhours line, some values are NA. You will come back to those in tab 3.

Step 4: how many people in each year?

  1. Fill in the blank with year and run the line:
    count(gss, year)
  2. Read the eight counts. One year has far more people than the others.
The GSS runs every two years, so the years go 2000, 2002, 2004, and so on. Most years have between about 2,000 and 2,800 people. 2006 has 4,510. A count tells you how many people the survey interviewed. It does not tell you why one year was larger. That is a question for the survey's documentation.

Step 5: keep only one year

  1. Fill in the blank with 2014. Numbers do not need quotation marks.
    gss_2014 <- filter(gss, year == 2014)
  2. Run it. Nothing prints. R stored the result under the name gss_2014, which now appears in the Environment pane.
  3. Fill in the next blank with gss_2014 and run nrow(gss_2014). Then run the count(gss_2014, marital) line.
Checkpoint. nrow(gss_2014) gives 2538, the same number Step 4 showed for 2014. The filter() function keeps the rows that match a rule. The double equals sign == asks "is this equal to?" A single = does something else in R, so a rule always uses two.

What the counts leave out: Steps 6 to 8

Every dataset has people with no answer recorded for some questions. Before you report anything from a variable, you need to know who has no answer and why. These steps look at two variables where the answer is "many people."

Step 6: the income categories

  1. Fill in the blank with rincome and run the line:
    count(gss, rincome)
  2. Read all 16 categories. Most are amounts of money. ("Lt $1000" means less than $1,000.) Four categories are not amounts of money: the first three and the last one.
No answer, Don't know, Refused
The person was asked about their income and did not give an amount.
Not applicable
The question did not apply to the person, so it was not asked. 7,043 people are in this category.
You will think about who the "Not applicable" people are in question 2 of the response sheet. A hint: rincome is income from the person's own work.

Step 7: who has no TV hours?

Before you run Step 7: out of all 21,483 people, what percent have no value recorded for TV hours?
10%
  1. Run count(gss, is.na(tvhours)). The is.na() function asks "is this value missing?" TRUE means missing. FALSE means a value is there.
  2. Fill in the blank in the next line with 2004. Run that line and the count() line below it.
  3. Change 2004 to 2014. Run both lines again. Compare the two years.
Checkpoint. In 2004, 1,913 of 2,812 people have no TV-hours value. In 2014, 869 of 2,538 do. The GSS does not ask every question of every person. Since 1988, many GSS questions have been asked of a randomly chosen part of each year's sample, often two-thirds. A missing value can mean the person was never asked, or that they were asked and gave no answer. The data on its own does not tell you which.

Step 8: chart the income categories
