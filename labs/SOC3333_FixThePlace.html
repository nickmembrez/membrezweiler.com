Thirteen chapters have been about people. Why they offend, how we measure it, what we do to them afterward. This last chapter changes the unit of analysis. It studies criminal events in space rather than criminal propensity in persons, and it asks a question none of the other thirteen asked: what if you could stop the event without ever identifying an offender? That turns out to be possible more often than the rest of the course would lead you to expect, and the evidence for it is unusually good. Nothing on this page is recorded and no guess is graded.

Crime concentrates at addresses, not in neighborhoods

Chapter 5 told you crime clusters. This chapter tells you how tightly, and the answer is tighter than almost anyone guesses. Researchers have counted crime at the level of the street segment, meaning one block face between two intersections, in city after city. Take a whole city, sort its street segments by how much crime each one produces, and start at the top. Across eight cities, between 2.1 and 6.0 percent of street segments accounted for 50 percent of crime, and between 0.4 and 1.6 percent accounted for 25 percent. Now the part that makes it a law rather than a fact. The Seattle segments held their rank for sixteen years while citywide crime fell by more than a fifth. The same blocks stayed hot and the same blocks stayed cold, through a crime decline, through changes in who lived there, through changes in policing. Whatever is producing crime at those addresses is a property of the addresses.
Why this reframes everything behind you. If crime concentrates that tightly at places, then a policy aimed at the average offender is aimed at the wrong unit. Chapters 8 through 11 asked what is different about the person. This chapter asks what is different about the corner, and it turns out you can answer that question without ever answering the first one. That is the whole argument of environmental criminology, and it is why the field sometimes calls itself crime science rather than criminology.

Two kinds of hot place, which need opposite fixes.

Crime generators gather large numbers of ordinary people for ordinary reasons. A mall, a stadium, a transit hub. Crime arrives with the foot traffic rather than because anyone sought the place out, so the fix is crowd and flow management.

Crime attractors are places offenders travel to because of the opportunities there. An unguarded lot, an open-air market for something illegal. The fix is removing the opportunity, because managing the crowd does nothing when the crowd is the point.

Broken windows is three separate things

Almost every argument about broken windows goes wrong in the same way, which is that people argue about all three at once. Keep them apart and the evidence becomes readable.

One, the theory. Visible disorder signals that nobody is in charge of a place. Unrepaired windows, blight, and unchecked incivility communicate an absence of guardianship, and that invites more serious offending. Physical disorder and social disorder both count.

Two, the policing style. Order maintenance and zero tolerance. Arrest for the small things so the big things never arrive. New York adopted a version of this at scale in the 1990s and it was widely credited with the crime decline there, a claim that remains contested because crime fell in cities that did nothing of the kind.

Three, the backlash. Aggressive order maintenance fell hardest on poor communities of color, produced the stop-and-frisk era, and fed exactly the labeling machine Chapter 11 described. Perceptions of what counts as disorder track racial composition, which means the input to the whole strategy is contaminated. George Kelling, who co-wrote the original article, objected that the theory had asked for order and had been turned into mass arrest.

So does policing disorder reduce crime? A 2024 systematic review pooled 54 tests and answered it two ways, once for community and problem-solving approaches that fix the disorder, and once for aggressive order maintenance that arrests people associated with it. Predict both before you look.

Read the two numbers together. The theory survives and the policing style does not. Disorder does matter, and dealing with it does reduce crime, but the version that works is the one that repairs the place rather than the one that arrests the people standing near it. You can fix broken windows with glaziers instead of handcuffs, and on this evidence the glaziers work better.
The methodological caution. Studies of a policing style are not randomized in the way the next tab's experiment is. Departments choose where to concentrate, and places chosen for intensive attention differ from places not chosen. The review handles this as carefully as the literature allows, and it is still weaker evidence than a trial. Notice that the weaker evidence favors the approach with the smaller civil liberties cost, which is a convenient result and therefore one to hold at arm's length.

The best experiment in the course

Philadelphia has tens of thousands of vacant lots. In 2013 researchers took 541 of them, grouped them into 110 geographically contiguous clusters, and randomly assigned each cluster to one of three conditions. Randomly. This is the Chapter 2 gold standard, applied to city blocks instead of people, and it is why this study carries more weight than anything else in the chapter.

The three arms. Full greening: remove the trash and debris, grade the land, plant grass and a few trees, install a low wooden fence, and maintain it. Mowing and trash cleanup only: the cheap version, no planting and no fence. And a no-intervention control, left exactly as it was. Outcomes came from police records and from repeated interviews with 445 residents living nearby, measured before and after.

Nobody was arrested and nobody was treated. No offender was identified, deterred, incapacitated, rehabilitated, or reintegrated. The place changed, and the events stopped happening there. Every theory in Chapters 6 through 13 needs a person to act on. This one does not, and that is the argument the chapter is making.
An honesty note you should carry into the forum. The 29 percent figure is the effect on gun violence around greened lots in neighborhoods below the poverty line, which is a specific subgroup and the largest effect the trial produced. A later analysis by the same research group, looking at shootings citywide rather than in that subgroup, reported smaller reductions of about 7 percent for greening and about 9 percent for mowing and trash cleanup. Both findings are real and they are not in conflict, because they answer different questions. If you cite the 29 percent, say which condition and which neighborhoods it refers to. Reporting the biggest number in a study without its qualifier is the single most common way a good finding turns into a bad claim.
And notice which arm was cheapest. In the citywide shooting analysis the mowing and trash cleanup arm performed at least as well as full greening. Mowing a lot costs almost nothing next to a year of anything discussed in Chapter 13. Whatever your view of punishment, the cost comparison is not close.

Situational prevention is offense-specific by design. There is no general fix. The measure that stops bar fights does nothing about catalytic converter theft, and the measure that stops converter theft does nothing about phishing. That is a feature rather than a limitation, because it forces you to analyze one problem at one place rather than to legislate against a category.

The design tradition, in one paragraph

Crime prevention through environmental design, usually shortened to CPTED, rests on three ideas. Natural surveillance means arranging space so that ordinary people can see it without trying, through windows facing the street, clear sightlines, and lighting. Access control means using real and symbolic gateways to channel movement. Territoriality means designing space so that it reads as belonging to somebody. Oscar Newman's insight was that architecture can do a guardian's job, permanently and without wages.

And the ethical test this chapter cannot avoid. The same logic produces hostile architecture: spikes under bridges, armrests bisecting benches, boulders under overpasses. Those designs do not prevent a crime event. They relocate an unwanted person, almost always an unhoused one, and they do it without anyone having to make an argument in public. The test to apply is simple. Does this design prevent a criminal event, or does it just move a person somebody would rather not see? One of those is crime science. The other is the conflict-theory question from Week 1, poured in concrete.

Will it just move somewhere else?

This is the objection every student raises, and it deserves a serious answer rather than a reassurance. If prevention only squeezes the balloon, nothing has been gained and the resources were wasted. Crime could displace in six ways: to another place, another time, another target, another tactic, another offense, or to a different offender who steps into the gap.

Researchers reviewed 102 evaluations of situational prevention and examined every observation where displacement or diffusion could have been detected. Guess what share of those observations showed displacement of any kind.

Diffusion of benefits is the finding nobody expects. Crime often falls in the surrounding area too, not only in the place that was treated, and it happens about as often as displacement does. Offenders do not know exactly where the intervention stops, so uncertainty spreads outward from it. The 2024 disorder policing review found the same thing, with effects spilling over into surrounding areas rather than pushing crime into them.
Why the balloon metaphor fails. It assumes a fixed quantity of crime looking for somewhere to go, which assumes an offender with a fixed determination to offend that day. Routine activity theory from Chapter 7 says otherwise. Many offenses are opportunistic, and when the easy opportunity is gone a good share of people simply do not go looking for a harder one. Displacement is real and it is partial. It is a reason to measure the surrounding area, not a reason to do nothing.

Take this to the forum

This is the last forum of the semester, and it asks you to do the thing the whole course has been building toward: take the offense you have studied all term and design a response to it that prevents harm without manufacturing a criminal.

Your forum prompt. Design one place-based intervention for the offense in your term paper. Name the place, name the mechanism you are using, and name the displacement objection you would expect. Then answer it.

Four things that separate an adequate post from a strong one.

One. Pick a real place, not a category. Not neighborhoods with high burglary rates, but the kind of specific setting your offense actually happens in. Chapter 14's whole claim is that the unit of analysis is the address. If your intervention could be described without naming a place, it is not a place-based intervention.

Two. Say which leg of the triangle you are removing. Motivated offender, suitable target, or capable guardian. The tab above walks through ten examples. If your measure does not obviously attack one of the three, work out which one it is before you post, because that is the question a reply will ask you.

Three. Take the displacement objection seriously. The honest answer is not that displacement never happens. It is that it is partial, that diffusion of benefits is about as common, and that you would want to measure the surrounding area rather than assume either way. Say what you would measure.

Four. Apply the ethics test. Does your design prevent an event, or does it move a person? If your intervention would make life harder for people who are not offending, say so and defend it, or change it. That distinction is the last thing this course has to teach you and it is the one most likely to matter after the semester ends.

If your offense does not happen in a physical place. Fraud, identity theft, and most cybercrime still have settings, they are just not street corners. A platform, a payment flow, a login page, a customer service script that can be socially engineered. Routine activity theory made the move online intact: two-factor authentication is guardianship and a password manager is target hardening. Name the digital setting with the same specificity you would give a corner.
