Women are twice as likely as men to report this fear, and slightly less likely to be violently victimized overall. That is the classic fear paradox, and it has real answers rather than being a puzzle about irrationality. Fear tracks the severity of the feared outcome as well as its probability, and sexual violence is both far more common against women and far worse to contemplate. Fear also tracks perceived vulnerability, meaning your ability to escape or resist. And fear is self-fulfilling in a specific way: people who are afraid restrict their routines, which lowers their exposure, which lowers their victimization rate. The behavior that fear produces is part of why the rate looks the way it does.

One honest complication, because your textbook states the second paradox more confidently than the data supports. The familiar version says older people fear most and are victimized least, while young people fear least and are victimized most. The age half of that is shakier than the gender half. In Gallup's own multi-year analysis, adults under 30 reported MORE fear than adults aged 30 to 64, and Gallup noted that fear is generally higher among the groups most likely to be victimized, including low-income respondents. Learn the paradox as it is stated in the chapter, because that is what the exam covers, and know that the age version of it is contested.

Read the whole column before you describe this. The bottom category is dramatically higher than everything above it. But the categories above it are not neatly ordered, and the highest income group is not the safest. A student who writes that victimization falls steadily as income rises has described a gradient the data does not show. What the data shows is a cliff at the bottom and rough flatness above it, which is a different claim and a more defensible one.

Fewer than half of violent victimizations and fewer than a third of property victimizations reached the police. Every one of those unreported events is a real person who was harmed and who does not appear in any police statistic, any clearance rate, or any arrest figure. When you read the Chapter 6 clearance numbers, remember that they are a percentage of the crimes police knew about, which is itself about half.

The finding students find hardest to accept

Victimology began badly. Early researchers built typologies of victims and coined terms like victim precipitation, meaning the victim used force first, victim facilitation, meaning carelessness made the offense easier, and victim provocation. Some of that was analytically useful. Wolfgang's homicide studies found that roughly one in four killings began with the victim's own aggression, which is a real feature of how lethal violence happens. Then Amir applied the precipitation framework to sexual violence, and the field had to reckon with what it had built.

What the field kept and what it dropped. Kept: the analytical insight that most criminal events are interactions between people rather than things one person does to a passive object. Dropped: the moral claim that a victim therefore shares blame. Those two are separable, and holding them apart is the entire skill this section is asking for. Explaining how an event unfolded is not the same as distributing responsibility for it.

The overlap

People who offend are victimized at much higher rates than people who do not, and people who are victimized offend at higher rates than people who are not. It is the same people, in the same places, at the same hours, doing the same things. Routine activity theory explains this without any difficulty: a lifestyle that puts you in unsupervised settings with motivated offenders puts you there as a potential target too. The triangle does not care which role you occupy.

Say this carefully, and understand why the care is required. The overlap is a statement about rates in populations. It is not a statement that any particular victim brought it on themselves, and it does not license the inference from someone having been harmed to something about their character. This is the same discipline Chapter 5 demanded about race and Chapter 10 demanded about discrimination: the mechanism is exposure and routine, not desert. A criminologist who cannot state the overlap without implying blame has not understood it.

Two more patterns worth carrying to the exam

The offender is usually known
Much violence comes from partners, family, and acquaintances rather than strangers. The stranger-danger picture is the exception, not the rule, and it shapes both fear and policy in the wrong direction.
Repeat and polyvictimization
A small share of people absorb a large share of all victimization. This is the Chapter 5 concentration finding, applied to victims instead of places, and it has an obvious policy implication.
What follows from that
Prevention aimed at people who have already been victimized prevents more harm per dollar than prevention spread evenly, because the next victimization is disproportionately likely to happen to them. Chapter 14 builds on this.
