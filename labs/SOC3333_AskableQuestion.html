What a research question is for

A research question is not a topic with a question mark on the end. A topic is a thing you are interested in. A question is a specific claim you do not yet know the truth of, phrased so that evidence could settle it. The difference is what separates a paper that goes somewhere from a paper that circles.

The test, in one sentence. Could two careful researchers, looking at the same body of evidence, agree that your question has been answered? If the answer is no, you do not have a research question yet. You have either a topic, an opinion, or a project that would take a career.

Four things a usable question has

One. It names a specific thing, not a category. Crime is not a subject. Burglary is a subject. Residential burglary in the daytime is a question waiting to happen. Broad categories do have research literatures, and that is not what is wrong with them. The trouble is that a literature on property crime covers a dozen offenses at once, so only a slice of it is ever about yours. Narrow until most of what you find speaks to your question, and then stop. You do not have to reach the narrowest possible thing.

Two. It asks about a relationship, a pattern, or an explanation. Good questions usually take one of a few shapes. What explains X? Who experiences X, and why them? Does Y reduce X? How has X been measured, and what does that measurement miss? Notice that all four can be answered wrongly, which is what makes them answerable at all.

Three. Evidence could change the answer. If no possible finding would make you say the opposite, the question is not empirical. That does not make it a bad question. It makes it a question for a different assignment, because this paper is a review of research findings.

Four. Somebody has already studied it. This is a mini-literature review, so a question with no literature behind it cannot be written. You will find this out fast in the library. If three searches turn up nothing, the question is not wrong, it is just not answerable in this format.

A preliminary question is allowed to be wrong. Part 1 says preliminary, and it means it. Almost every real project changes its question once the researcher reads the literature and discovers that the interesting disagreement is somewhere they did not expect. Changing your question between Part 1 and Part 2 is a sign the reading worked. What you cannot do is keep a question you already know is unanswerable and hope nobody notices.
How this question rules the next four parts. Part 2 defines and measures the thing your question is about. Part 3 gives its patterns. Part 4 explains it with theory. Part 5 puts all of that into one narrative and says what is still unknown. If your question is vague, every one of those parts inherits the vagueness and you will feel it in October. If it is sharp, each part has an obvious job.

Is this the right kind of source?

Peer-reviewed is necessary and it is not sufficient. Two things have to be true. The journal has to be in criminology, criminal justice, or sociology, and social psychology counts when the journal itself is about crime, law, or the legal system. The article itself has to report research. A superb journal in the wrong field is the wrong source, and so is the wrong kind of piece inside the right journal.

How to check, in ninety seconds. Find the article. Note the journal name. Search that name plus the words aims and scope, or go to the publisher page and look for the tab that says About or Aims and Scope. Read the paragraph the editors wrote about what they publish. That paragraph is the journal telling you, in its own words, what it is for. You are looking for crime, offending, victimization, the legal system, or criminal justice to appear in it as the subject rather than as an example.

The question underneath all seven. Ask what the authors did and what they found. A source that reports no study, and reviews no body of studies, is not a research article however good it is and however respectable the journal around it is. That one question sorts the hard cases faster than any checklist.
Two warning signs worth knowing. First, if you cannot find an aims and scope statement at all, or the statement covers every field at once, you are probably looking at a predatory journal that publishes for a fee without real review. Second, a magazine written for practitioners is not a journal, however useful it is. If there is no volume and issue number and no reference list at the end of the article, stop and find something else.

APA, down to the punctuation

The references page is worth points, and it is the easiest place in the whole assignment to collect them, because the rules are fixed and public. Here is a correct journal article reference in APA 7th edition with every element labeled.

Correct Pager, D. (2003). The mark of a criminal record. American Journal of Sociology, 108(5), 937–975.
Read it element by element. Last name, comma, initial with a period. Year in parentheses, then a period. Article title in sentence case, meaning only the first word and any proper nouns are capitalized, then a period. Journal name in title case and italics, then a comma. Volume number, also italic, then the issue number in parentheses and not italic, then a comma. Page range with an en dash, then a period. If the article has a DOI, it goes last as a full link in the form https://doi.org/ followed by the number, with no period after it.

The two rules that catch most people. Sentence case on the article title and title case on the journal name feels backward, and it is the single most common error in submitted work. Second, the ampersand is used inside a reference and inside a parenthetical citation, but the word "and" is used when you name the authors in your own sentence. So it is (Guerette & Bowers, 2009), and it is "Guerette and Bowers found that ..."
Use the official guidance rather than a citation generator. Generators get the italics and the capitalization wrong constantly, and the error is yours once you submit it.
