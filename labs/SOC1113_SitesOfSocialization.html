Somebody built the person you are, and it was not just you. Sociologists call the builders agents of socialization: family, school, peers, media, and religion. Every number on this page is a published measurement of how much of American childhood each agent claims, and the numbers have been moving for two generations. On four of the five tabs the key number is hidden until you commit a guess, so guess first. Then ask the question this page is really about: who is raising Americans now, and who was doing it when your grandparents were young?

The five builders. Four numbers are hidden until you guess them.

The agents do not cooperate. They compete, and the mix is shifting. Open each tab, make your guess, and the card above fills in with the published number. When all five are showing, go to the last tab and rank the builders.

Family: the first agent

The family gets you first. Language, class position, first norms, and your earliest sense of self all arrive through it, and you do not choose it. The data show the form changing, not the function.

School: the first institution you meet alone

School is where a child first faces an institution without family in the room, and it teaches far more than the syllabus admits. The big number on this tab is arithmetic, not a survey, so instead of guessing it you get to set the assumptions yourself and watch the total move.

What school teaches beyond the syllabus: sociologists call it the hidden curriculum. Punctuality, standing in line, raising your hand for permission to speak, deference to adult authority, being graded and ranked, treating time in blocks. Nobody posts these lessons on the board. Everyone learns them. Push the sliders to their lowest settings and the total still lands in the thousands of hours. That is the point: thousands of hours of rehearsal for institutional life, whatever the exact assumptions.

The hours figure is stated arithmetic with its assumptions shown, not a measurement. Requirements vary by state. The default settings, 180 days at 6.5 hours for 13 years, are the ones used on the lineup card.

Media: the newest agent, and the only one that never closes

For most of history the media agent was a shared living-room screen with a schedule. It is now a personalized feed that knows its audience one person at a time. These are the 2024 numbers for American teenagers.

What the feed teaches: how to look, what to want, how to argue, what counts as success, and how a self gets performed for an audience, which is next week's chapter. The sociological point is not that this is good or bad. It is that an agent as powerful as family or school now operates continuously, personally, and with no adult in the room.

Source: Pew Research Center, Teens, Social Media and Technology 2024 (survey of U.S. teens 13 to 17, fall 2024).

Within living memory, two of the oldest agents lost a large share of their hours and one brand-new agent moved in. The generalized other, the internalized voice of everybody, is being assembled from different sources than it was for your grandparents. What that produces is one of the open questions of current sociology, and you are the data.

Questions to take with you

  1. Use the ranking panel above. Where do your two lists differ most, and what happened between their childhood and yours to cause the move?
  2. The agents compete. Name one concrete lesson where two of your agents taught you opposite things, and which one won.
  3. Peer time in person fell by more than two thirds for your age group, and peer socialization obviously did not stop. What changed about how peers socialize each other when it moved onto platforms?
  4. The hidden curriculum card lists what school teaches without saying so. Name one hidden lesson from your own schooling that took years to notice.
  5. If congregations taught moral vocabulary and community for most of American history, what is teaching those now for the 43% of young adults outside any religion? Be specific.
  6. Every number here is a country-level average. Which of these trends does not describe your own life, and what does that mismatch tell you about averages?
