How couples actually meet

Stanford's How Couples Meet and Stay Together survey has asked American couples the same question for years: how did you two meet? Before the chart appears, make a call about the most recent data. The options are alphabetical.

By 2017, what was the number one way heterosexual couples met?

Share of couples who met each way, 1995 and 2017

Couples can name more than one way, so the columns do not sum to 100.

Every traditional matchmaker is in decline. Friends, family, church, school, and the workplace all introduced fewer couples in 2017 than in 1995, and one channel replaced them. Sociologists call the old pattern intermediation: your network vetted your partners before you met them. The apps removed the intermediaries. Here is the part worth sitting with: homogamy survived. People on the apps still overwhelmingly match with people of similar education and class. The matchmaker changed. The marriage market's rules mostly did not.

Source: Rosenfeld, Thomas, and Hausen, Disintermediating your friends (PNAS 2019), Table S3, heterosexual couples. Bar or restaurant figures include couples who first connected elsewhere, including online, before meeting there.

The autopsy: family structure and crime

You have heard the claim. Kids from single-parent homes turn to crime, so family breakdown explains the crime rate. It is one of the most repeated family claims in American politics, and it is exactly the kind of claim this course taught you to cut open in Chapter 2. Work the steps in order. No skipping ahead.

First, predict: when researchers compare adolescents from single-parent and two-parent families, is there a raw statistical association with criminal involvement?
1The association is real

A systematic review of 48 studies (Kroese and colleagues, 2021) finds that growing up in a single-parent family is associated with an elevated risk of criminal involvement in adolescence. Honest sociology does not deny an inconvenient correlation. It asks the next question.

2What else differs between these families?

A roughly five-fold poverty gap (Census, 2024), before you count neighborhood, school quality, parental time, and the conflict that usually precedes a divorce. Comparing single-parent and married-couple households is never a comparison of family structure alone. Chapter 2 vocabulary: the claim is loaded with confounders, and families are not randomly assigned to their structures. That is a selection effect.

3Control for what parents actually do, and watch the gap move

Demuth and Brown (2004) tested this on a national adolescent sample. Delinquency is higher in single-parent families, but the differences shrink once you account for family processes: closeness, involvement, supervision, and monitoring. And the difference between single-father and single-mother families disappears entirely once processes are controlled. What predicts delinquency is what parents do and what their circumstances let them do, far more than how many adults are on the lease.

4The verdict

The correlation is real and modest. The causal story told on cable news is not established: the Kroese review itself concludes that more research is needed to separate the effects of divorce, parental death, and being born to a single parent. Family structure in these studies stands in for money, time, conflict, neighborhood, and stress. Blaming the structure lets everything it stands in for off the hook.

And one Chapter 8 question to carry out the door: which families get labeled as the crime problem, and which confounders, poverty, neighborhood, policing, never make it into the headline? A charged correlation plus a ready-made villain is how moral entrepreneurs work. Now you can take one apart.
