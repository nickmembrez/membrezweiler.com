The difference between stapling and weaving

The most common way Part 5 goes wrong is not bad writing. It is four good papers placed end to end with new headings on top. A reader can tell within a page, because the seams show: the same study gets introduced twice, the tone shifts, and nothing in section three depends on anything in section two. A literature review is not a container for four papers. It is one argument about a body of research, and the four papers are its raw material.

The test for whether you have woven it. Pick any paragraph in the middle of your draft. Could you delete it and have the next paragraph still make sense? If yes, you have a list. In a review, paragraphs depend on the ones before them, because the point being built is cumulative rather than sequential.

A structure that works

Open with the puzzle, not the offense. Do not begin by defining your offense type. Begin with what is genuinely unresolved or surprising about it, and let the definition arrive while you are explaining why the puzzle matters. Your Part 1 has the material for this, but it is almost never the first paragraph of Part 1.

Establish the shape of the phenomenon before explaining it. How much of it happens, to whom, measured how, and what the measurement misses. This is Parts 1 and 2 doing the work Chapters 2 through 5 taught, and it has to come before theory, because a theory has to explain a pattern and the reader needs to see the pattern first.

Organize the theory section by argument, not by theory. This is the single biggest upgrade available to most drafts. Rather than a paragraph on strain, then one on control, then one on learning, organize around the questions the theories disagree about. Where they agree, say so and move on quickly. Where they conflict, that is a section, and the evidence that adjudicates it is the point of the section.

Bring the response in as a test, not an appendix. What has been tried against your offense, and what happened? Chapters 13 and 14 gave you the vocabulary and the evidence standards. An intervention that failed is evidence about the theory behind it, so this section belongs to your argument rather than sitting after it.

Close on what is not known. The next tab is about this, because it is the part most drafts fumble and the part that most distinguishes a review from a summary.

About incorporating the feedback. Revised means changed. If a comment on Part 2 said your sample description was thin and the sentence appears in Part 5 unchanged, that is visible, and it costs more than the original error did. Work through the four sets of comments before you start assembling rather than after, because some of them will change the structure and not just the sentences.

Writing the part about what nobody knows

Most drafts handle gaps in one sentence: more research is needed. That sentence is true of every topic ever studied, which is what makes it worthless. A real gaps section names something specific that is missing, says why its absence matters for what we can currently claim, and suggests what would fill it. Here are six places gaps reliably hide. Read them against your own topic and at least two will apply.

One. The measurement gap. Which instrument produced most of what we know, and what is that instrument structurally unable to see? Chapter 2 gave you this. If your offense is mostly known through police records, everything unreported is invisible, and any claim about trends is a claim about reporting as much as about behavior.
Two. The population gap. Who was studied and who was not? A literature built on convicted samples cannot tell you about people who offend and are never caught, which for most offense types is the majority of them.
Three. The correlation gap. Which of the relationships you have described are established as causal and which are associations that have been repeated so often they feel causal? Naming even one of these correctly will put your review ahead of most.
Four. The mechanism gap. Sometimes we know an intervention works and not why. Chapter 13 gave you the clean example, where restorative programs reduce reoffending while the remorse mechanism they claim to work through does not show up in measurements. If your literature has one of these, it is worth a paragraph.
Five. The context gap. Where was the research done, and would it hold elsewhere? A finding established in three large cities is not yet a finding about rural counties, and the theory usually does not tell you which way it should go.
Six. The time gap. How old is the core evidence, and what has changed since? For anything touching technology, drug markets, or policing practice, a literature centered on studies from twenty years ago is describing a different world.
Say it in the register of a scholar, not a critic. A gaps section is not a complaint about researchers. Every one of these gaps exists because of a real constraint: money, access, ethics, or the calendar. Naming the constraint alongside the gap is what makes the paragraph sound like it was written by somebody who understands how research gets made.

Fourteen chapters, one page

The final is cumulative with emphasis on the last two chapters. This is every unit of the course reduced to the thing you would need to reconstruct it. Read a row, look away, and say the rest of it out loud. What you cannot say is tonight's work.

The fastest way to sort a scenario question. Ask what kind of claim you are being handed. A claim about how much crime there is belongs to Chapters 2 through 5. A claim about why someone offends belongs to Chapters 6 through 10. A claim about what happens after they are caught belongs to Chapters 11 through 13. A claim about a place belongs to Chapter 14. That one question eliminates most of the answer options before you have thought about the content at all.

How to study for a cumulative final

Fourteen chapters is too much to reread and you do not have to. A cumulative exam with an emphasis section is testing whether the course cohered, which is a different thing from whether you remember all of it.

Start with the emphasis, not the beginning. Chapters 13 and 14 carry the extra weight and they are the freshest, which means they are the cheapest points available. Do those first while they are still cheap, then spend what is left on the rest.
Use the cheat sheet slides as the spine. Every theory unit ended with a table. Those tables are the course compressed by the person writing the exam, which makes them the highest-value pages you have. Read across a row and say the missing cells out loud before you look.
Study the conflicts, not the definitions. Definitions are worth a point each. The places where two theories predict opposite things are worth far more, because that is what a scenario question is built out of. Selection against socialization. Certainty against severity. Retribution capping a sentence against incapacitation extending it. Explaining a pattern against blaming the person in it.
Practice retrieving, not rereading. Rereading a chapter feels productive and mostly is not. Close the book and write down everything you can about one theory, then open it and find what you missed. The gap between those two lists is your actual study list, and nothing else on the page will find it for you.
Learn the numbers that carry an argument. You do not need every statistic in the course. You need the handful where the number is the point: how much crime the top few percent of street segments carry, what happened to callbacks when a record was added, what share of the corrections budget goes to programs, what a randomized trial did to gun violence by mowing a lot. Those are the ones a short answer question can be built around.
And one thing worth saying at the end of a semester. If you can read a crime statistic and ask which instrument produced it and what that instrument cannot see, you have the thing this course was actually for. That skill does not expire when the exam window closes, and it will be useful to you in arguments that have nothing to do with crime.
