Property crime outnumbers violent crime by roughly five to one. About 17 of every 100 offenses in these two families are violent. That ratio is close to the reverse of what the news, politics, and your own sense of the word crime suggest. Hold that mismatch. One death, six possible charges

Homicide means a human being killed another human being. That is all it means. Everything after that is sorting, and the variable doing the sorting is intent. Here is the ladder, from most culpable to least.

First-degree murderIntentional and premeditated. The killing was planned, however briefly.
Second-degree murderIntentional but not premeditated. The intent formed in the moment.
Felony murderA death during the commission of another felony. Intent to kill is not required.
Voluntary manslaughterIntentional, but in the heat of passion after adequate provocation.
Involuntary manslaughterUnintentional, caused by recklessness or criminal negligence.
Justifiable homicideLegally sanctioned. Not a crime at all, and excluded from crime counts.

Five deaths follow. Pick the rung you think fits, then reveal. These are teaching cases rather than real prosecutions, and the fine print matters: statutes differ by state, and in practice a prosecutor charges and a jury decides.

Notice what did the work. In every case the physical act was similar, a person died at another person's hands, and the legal category moved anyway. What moved it was a mental state you cannot observe directly and have to infer from circumstances: planning, provocation, recklessness, the presence of another felony. That is why homicide law is really a typology of minds rather than a typology of acts.

And notice the felony murder rung. It is the one rule on this ladder that convicts people of murder without any intent to kill, and in many states it reaches accomplices who were never in the room. Supporters argue it deters people from committing dangerous felonies at all. Critics argue it punishes an outcome rather than a choice, and several states have narrowed it in recent years. You do not have to resolve that. You do have to notice that a category, not a fact, is doing the deciding.

Categories follow the standard homicide typology used in criminology textbooks, including Zito, Engaged Criminology, Chapter 3. Definitions vary by jurisdiction, and these vignettes are written to illustrate the categories rather than to predict how any particular state would charge them.

Robbery or burglary? The most common mistake in the field

Almost everyone uses these two words interchangeably. They are not close. Robbery takes property from a person by force or threat of force, so a victim is present and it is a violent crime. Burglary is the unlawful entry of a structure with the intent to commit a crime inside, so it is a property crime and nobody has to be home. Six situations. Sort each one.

The rule underneath all six. Ask one question first: was a person confronted with force or the threat of it? If yes, you are in robbery, and it is filed with violent crime no matter what got taken. If no, and someone entered a structure unlawfully, you are in burglary, and it is filed with property crime no matter how frightening it was. If neither, you are usually in larceny-theft, the largest crime category in the country and the one nobody talks about.

Why a vocabulary drill is not a vocabulary drill. These words decide which national number an event lands in. Two nearly identical nights, one where the residents were away and one where they were home and confronted, split into two different families of crime and two different public conversations. When you read that violent crime rose in a city, some of that movement is a change in what happened, and some of it is a change in which box events fell into.

The Kia case: when one category breaks the pattern

Between 2019 and 2023, while most crime was flat or falling, the national rate of motor vehicle theft incidents rose from 199.4 per 100,000 people to 283.5, an increase of 42.1 percent. Then it collapsed. This tab is about why, and the answer is not that a new kind of person appeared.

What actually happened

A design decision met a social network. Certain Kia models built from 2011 to 2021 and Hyundai models from 2016 to 2021 were sold in the United States without engine immobilizers, an anti-theft device standard on most other vehicles. That vulnerability sat there quietly for years. In July 2022 a video demonstrating how to start those cars with a USB connector spread on social media, and by that autumn the method had spread nationwide. Los Angeles recorded an 85 percent increase in thefts of Kia and Hyundai vehicles in 2022 compared with 2021. Chicago thefts of those makes rose more than ninefold. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration attributes eight deaths to the trend.

Then the vulnerability was patched. Hyundai began releasing a free anti-theft software update in February 2023 and finished rolling it out to eligible vehicles that June. Motor vehicle theft fell an estimated 18.6 percent in 2024, and the insurance industry counted the largest annual drop in stolen vehicles in forty years. Thefts of the affected Hyundai and Kia models fell by roughly a third in a single year.

Read this carefully before you use it. The timing lines up and the mechanism is plausible, but the software patch was not the only thing happening. Pandemic-era disruptions were unwinding, parts supply was recovering, and nearly every crime category was falling at the same time. The honest claim is that a specific, cheap change in the physical world coincided with a sharp reversal in one specific offense while other offenses fell for their own reasons. Chapter 2 taught you that coincidence in time is association, not proof of cause.

Why this belongs in a chapter about typologies. Motor vehicle theft is filed under property crime, and property crime is supposed to be boring background. For four years it was the most interesting number in American criminology, and the explanation had nothing to do with a new kind of offender, a new kind of poverty, or a new moral climate. It had to do with which cars could be started with a USB cable. Chapter 14 will call this opportunity, and this is your first serious encounter with the idea that crime can be designed out of a situation rather than argued out of a person.

Public-order crime: who decides?

The third family is the strange one. Public-order offenses have no complaining victim, so nobody calls the police to report one. They enter the data only when officers go looking. That single fact changes what the numbers mean.

More than one in five drug arrests was for possessing marijuana, in a country where most states have legalized it in some form and roughly two thirds of the public supports legalizing it, which you saw in the Week 1 tool. The same act is a licensed retail purchase in one state and an arrest in another. Nothing about the substance changes at the state line. What changes is the filing system.

Now the measurement trap, and it is the important part. Because no victim reports these offenses, an arrest count for drugs measures police activity as much as it measures behavior. If a department assigns more officers to drug enforcement, drug crime rises in the data. If it reassigns them, drug crime falls. Neither number tells you whether more people used drugs. Compare that with homicide, where the offense is nearly impossible to hide. Two rows in the same federal table, two completely different relationships to reality.

The San Francisco opium ordinance of 1875, the Harrison Narcotics Act of 1914, national alcohol prohibition in 1920, the Marihuana Tax Act of 1937, and the Controlled Substances Act of 1970 each created crime by statute. Alcohol prohibition was then repealed, and marijuana is being unmade as a crime state by state right now. Chapter 3 has a name for the people who drive these campaigns in either direction: moral entrepreneurs. Take the Chapter 1 question with you. Does this history look more like a society converging on shared values, which is the consensus view, or like particular groups writing their interests into law, which is the conflict view? The strongest answer names specific evidence for one and admits what the other explains better.

Forum 3: your post

Write roughly 250 to 300 words for your initial post, then reply to two classmates. Check that week’s module in the LMS for the posting and reply deadlines.

  1. Pick any three offenses. Do not use my examples, and consider using the offense you are circling for your term paper as one of them.
  2. Classify each one three ways: by severity (felony, misdemeanor, or infraction), by victim type (against persons, property, public order, or the state), and by purpose (instrumental, meaning crime as a means to an end, or expressive, meaning crime as an end in itself).
  3. Defend your trickiest call. One of your nine judgments should have been genuinely hard. Name it, explain both readings, and say which you chose and why. "Both" is an acceptable answer only if you explain what the ambiguity reveals about the typology.
  4. Close with the payoff question: what does your hardest case show about the limits of sorting crime into boxes? Point at something concrete from this week rather than saying categories are complicated.

For your replies: take a run at a classmate's hardest call and argue the other side, using the definitions rather than intuition. If someone classified an offense as instrumental and you think it is expressive, say what evidence would settle it. Disagreement handled with definitions is exactly what this week is teaching.
